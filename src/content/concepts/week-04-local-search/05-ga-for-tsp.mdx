## The Simplest Explanation

Applying GA to TSP breaks immediately: a tour is a **permutation**, and swapping segments between two permutations usually produces invalid children (repeated or missing cities). The fixes come in two flavors:

1. Keep the natural **path representation** but invent permutation-safe crossovers (CX, PMX, OX)
2. Change the **encoding** so standard crossover just works (adjacency, ordinal)

<Callout type="tip" title="Remember This">
Path + CX/PMX/OX = valid-by-construction crossovers. Ordinal representation = make plain single-point crossover safe. Adjacency = fast next-city lookup but some strings aren't tours.
</Callout>

## Path Representation & Permutation-Safe Crossovers

Tour written as visit order: `C A B D` means C→A→B→D→C.

### Cycle Crossover (CX)

Find "cycles" of positions where the two parents' cities loop into each other: if position 1 holds O in P1 and A in P2, and some later position holds A in P1 and O in P2, those two positions form a cycle — following the swap from one returns you to the other.

- Child 1 takes **odd cycles** from P1, even cycles from P2
- Child 2 gets the opposite
- Every city appears exactly once by construction ✓

### Partially Mapped Crossover (PMX)

1. Copy a contiguous subtour from P1 into C1
2. For each conflicting remaining position, follow the partial mapping (P1-city ↔ P2-city) until an open slot is found
3. Unconflicting cities copied straight from P2

### Order Crossover (OX)

1. Copy a subtour from P1 into C1
2. Fill remaining positions with P2's cities **in the order they appear in P2**, skipping duplicates

Preserves relative ordering information from P2.

## Adjacency Representation

Index cities alphabetically; value at position i = the city visited **after** i.

- Tour A→H→K stores H at index A, K at index H.
- Pro: next-city lookup is O(1); heuristic crossover ("take whichever parent's edge is shorter") is natural
- Con: not every string decodes to one complete tour — premature small cycles can appear; alternating-edges crossover needs repair

## Ordinal Representation — The Clever One

Invented precisely so that **plain single-point crossover always yields a valid tour**.

Encode: keep an index list of remaining cities in alphabetical order. Walk the tour; at each step record the *current index* of the visited city, then remove it from the list.

Tour `O D G ...`:
1. Index `[A,B,C,...]`: O sits at position 15 → write 15, remove O
2. Next D: now at position 4 → write 4, remove D
3. Next G: position 6 → write 6, remove G
4. Continue; final city always writes 1

Resulting sequence decreases toward 1. Any single-point crossover over two such sequences still decodes to a valid permutation — no repair needed.

<Callout type="warning" title="Tradeoff">
Encoding/decoding is more work, and ordinal numbers don't correspond to cities intuitively — harder to reason about what crossover does to tour structure.
</Callout>

## Comparison

| Representation | Standard 1-point crossover? | Notes |
|----------------|----------------------------|-------|
| Path + CX / PMX / OX | ✗ (needs special operators) | preserves subtours / relative order |
| Adjacency | ✗ | O(1) lookup; risk of sub-cycles |
| **Ordinal** | **✓ safe by design** | encode/decode overhead |

<Accordion title="Common Exam Pitfalls">
1. **Mixing up CX and OX**: CX alternates whole cycles between parents; OX copies one segment then preserves P2's ORDER for the rest. PMX resolves conflicts via the partial mapping.
2. **Adjacency validity**: adjacency strings are not all tours — a child containing a short cycle (A→B→A) is invalid and needs repair.
3. **Ordinal decode direction**: remember you REMOVE each city as you go — later indices shrink accordingly.
</Accordion>
