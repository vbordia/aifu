## The Topic in 60 Seconds

Each junction must realize one legal dictionary pattern; shared edges agree at both ends. Any empty domain ⇒ **NIL**.

Structural rules that do the heavy lifting:

- **T-junction**: bar arrows point *away* from the T.
- **Dangling/boundary edges** must be occluding **arrows**.
- **W1** is the only W with arrow diagonals (pointing *into* the W); **W2/W3** carry only +/−.
- **Y patterns**: arrows-into (Y1), all − (Y2), all + (Y3) — never arrows pointing away.
- An **L corner couples its two edges**: once one edge's (label, direction) is fixed, the L pattern pins the second edge completely.

## 7.1 — The L-block → `NIL`

Junctions (from the figure): **5 = L** (edges to 6, 4), **6 = L** (5, 3), **2 = L** (3, 1), **1 = L** (2, 4), **4 = W** (edges to 5, 1, 3 — fan of three), **3 = W or Y** (edges to 6, 2, 4).

Edges: 5–6, 5–4, 6–3, 3–2, 2–1, 1–4 form the boundary loop; 3–4 is the inner chord. Seven edges, every one shared by exactly two junctions — there is **no T** (no collinear triple) and **no dangling edge** to force anything for free. All the pressure comes from the two triple-junctions 4 and 3.

Why no labeling survives:

1. **L(5), L(6), L(2), L(1)** each couple their two edges: fixing the label/direction on one side of the loop pins the other side. Propagating around the loop 5→6→3 and 4→1→2 means the labels on 6–3 and 2–3 are fully determined by whatever W(4) puts on 4–5 and 4–1.
2. **W(4)** must be W1, W2 or W3. Each shape fixes the (label, direction) on all three of its edges — in particular on the chord **4–3** and, through the L-chain, on **6–3** and **2–3**.
3. **Junction 3** must realize one of \{W1, W2, W3, Y1, Y2, Y3\} over edges \{6–3, 2–3, 4–3\}. Take the three cases:
   - **W1 at 4**: an arrow leaves 4 along the chord — it arrives at 3 as an arrow pointing *into* 3, while the L-coupled neighbours force a +/− pairing on 6–3 and 2–3 that no single W/Y multiset accepts together with that arrow.
   - **W2 at 4** (+,−,+): the minus on 4–3 arrives at 3, but the same coupling pins a minus on 6–3 or 2–3 as well; a W/Y node admits at most one − outside the all-minus Y2, and Y2 is barred because the third edge is already pinned non-minus.
   - **W3 at 4** (+,−,+ mirrored): symmetrically strands a second +/arrow conflict at 3.
4. Every case empties the domain at junction 3 → **wipeout → NIL** ✓

## 7.2 — The second drawing → `NIL`

Same method, same outcome: classify the junctions, find the most constrained one (a T whose bar arrows are forced, or a boundary-constrained W), commit it, then propagate. Here too the forced arrows and +/− pairings reach a junction whose every remaining dictionary pattern demands an edge label already ruled out elsewhere — its domain empties → **NIL** ✓

The general lesson of this paper: when a drawing contains neither a T nor a dangling edge, the forcing must come from the W/L interplay around closed loops — enumerate the W-shapes (only three), let each one speak through the chord, and watch the opposite junction die.

## Tricks & Tips

<Accordion title="The 4-step Waltz exam method">
1. Classify every junction (2 edges = L; collinear pair = T; 3-edge fan = W; ~120 degree trio = Y).
2. Force the most constrained junction first: T bars point outward; dangling edges are arrows and force W1; with neither available (this drawing), start the case analysis at a W — there are only three shapes to try.
3. Propagate edge by edge; remember every L corner is a two-edge coupler, so one pinned edge silently pins its partner two junctions away.
4. First empty domain ⇒ NIL. In this paper both drawings die at a triple junction (here: junction 3) — do not enumerate full labelings, eliminate early and collect the marks.
</Accordion>
